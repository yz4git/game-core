## Observation / Mechanism candidates

### 1. Physical controls can be part of the rules, not a skin

Observation:
Heavyweight Champ mapped high/low targeting and punching onto articulated glove-like controls. Hang-On made body lean itself steering input.

Mechanism:
When the player's physical action resembles the simulated action, the control explanation becomes partly embodied rather than symbolic.

Generalized Principle:
**A special controller is strongest when its physical affordance explains a meaningful game variable.**

Transfer:
Touch games can imitate this principle with drag direction, device tilt, two-thumb separation, pressure-like hold duration, or spatial gestures—but only when the gesture corresponds naturally to the game state.

Playtest Question:
Can a first-time observer predict what moving the control will do before being told?

---

### 2. Novel hardware needs mechanical neutral and recovery

Observation:
Heavyweight Champ's springs returned the controls to neutral. Physical arcade interfaces repeatedly depend on self-centering or obvious resting positions.

Mechanism:
A physical input that does not clearly return to a known state increases calibration errors, fatigue and uncertainty.

Generalized Principle:
**Every continuous physical control needs a legible neutral state and a fast recovery path.**

Transfer:
Virtual sticks, steering wheels and touch sliders should visually/similarly return to neutral and avoid hidden residual input.

Playtest Question:
After releasing the control, does the player immediately know whether input is zero?

---

### 3. Physical intensity should not encode essential precision alone

Observation:
The original Street Fighter deluxe cabinet measured strike force to select attack strength, while the later six-button scheme represented strengths discretely.

Mechanism:
Force input is expressive and theatrical, but human strength, cabinet calibration and repeated exertion introduce variance unrelated to tactical intent.

Generalized Principle:
**Use physical intensity for expression or coarse choice unless the system can reliably separate intention from body variance.**

Transfer:
On touch devices, avoid making swipe speed or press duration the sole way to choose precise competitive strength tiers unless calibration and accessibility alternatives exist.

Playtest Question:
Do different players intending the same action receive the same result reliably?

---

### 4. Special controls create spectator-readable intent

Observation:
Leaning a motorcycle cabinet, punching large pads, steering a wheel or operating paired levers exposes player intent to people standing nearby.

Mechanism:
The body becomes an external animation of the control state. Spectators can understand effort and direction without reading the HUD.

Generalized Principle:
**Public-play controls should make player intention visible outside the game screen when possible.**

Transfer:
For convention/mobile party modes, exaggerate avatar/controller feedback so observers can map visible player action to in-game consequence.

Playtest Question:
Can a spectator identify what the player just tried to do without seeing the player's fingers closely?

---

### 5. Arcade differentiation shifts when home hardware catches up

Observation:
SEGA's historical account describes motion cabinets in the mid-1980s and later polygon hardware as ways arcade experiences differentiated while home systems improved.

Mechanism:
When a previously exclusive technical feature becomes ordinary at home, public-play value must migrate to something harder to reproduce: physical scale, social presence, specialized input, shared spectacle, location or service.

Generalized Principle:
**Do not define platform differentiation by a temporary hardware advantage; define it by an experience bundle that remains hard to substitute.**

Transfer:
Web/mobile games should not compete only on graphics. Instant access, touch-native controls, link sharing, short-session social play and persistent community can be stronger platform-specific advantages.

Playtest Question:
If another platform matched the graphics tomorrow, what reason would remain to play this version?

---

### 6. Cabinet novelty must survive the second play

Observation:
Hang-On paired its ride-on novelty with line choice, acceleration and time performance; its appeal was not only sitting on a motorcycle-shaped cabinet.

Mechanism:
Physical spectacle attracts the first credit, but repeat play requires a learnable performance variable.

Generalized Principle:
**Novel input earns attention; skill expression earns repetition.**

Transfer:
Tilt, gyro, motion, unusual touch gestures and camera tricks should expose mastery curves rather than exist as one-off gimmicks.

Playtest Question:
After the novelty is understood, what measurable skill can improve over ten runs?

---

### 7. The same theme can support multiple control embodiments

Observation:
Hang-On existed in ride-on and sit-down forms, with related but not identical physical interaction.

Mechanism:
The fantasy can survive different input embodiments if the core decision variables remain intact.

Generalized Principle:
**Separate the fantasy-critical decisions from the hardware-specific gesture.**

Transfer:
Design touch/controller/keyboard mappings from the same semantic actions rather than mechanically translating button positions.

Playtest Question:
Across input methods, are players making the same important decisions even when gestures differ?

---

### 8. Motion feedback should communicate simulation state

Observation:
Thunder Blade linked lever motion to seat movement and used the cabinet to reinforce helicopter floating sensation.

Mechanism:
Physical movement can communicate acceleration, orientation and instability through another sensory channel.

Generalized Principle:
**Motion/haptics are most useful when they reveal state, not when they merely shake on every event.**

Transfer:
Use mobile haptics/camera response selectively for grip loss, collision severity, parry timing or vehicle load.

Playtest Question:
With the visual HUD reduced, can the player infer meaningful state from motion/haptic feedback?

---

### 9. Shared hardware can create rivalry without changing the core task

Observation:
Air Rescue's twin cabinet let two players perform the same rescue/shooting task while comparing rescued-person counts.

Mechanism:
A shared objective plus a visible personal contribution produces simultaneous cooperation and competition.

Generalized Principle:
**Co-op becomes socially richer when individual contribution is legible without undermining the shared objective.**

Transfer:
For local multiplayer, show assists, rescues, saves, passes or role contributions rather than only final kills.

Playtest Question:
Can each player tell what they contributed while still caring about the team's result?

---

### 10. Post-session social feedback can extend a short game

Observation:
Magical Truck Adventure used two-player cooperation and reported a compatibility-style result after play.

Mechanism:
A short run can generate a conversation artifact after the mechanical session ends.

Generalized Principle:
**Public/local multiplayer benefits from a post-run summary that describes the relationship between players, not only the score.**

Transfer:
Measure synchronized actions, saves, passes, risk-sharing, distance kept, or complementary roles and turn them into playful run summaries.

Playtest Question:
Does the result screen make players talk about what happened together?

---

### 11. Broadening the arcade audience changes game form, not just marketing

Observation:
SEGA's historical account links prize and medal games with increased participation by women, couples and casual visitors and a shift in arcade atmosphere.

Mechanism:
Lower rule burden, recognizable physical goals and social accessibility change who can enter the activity without prior genre literacy.

Generalized Principle:
**Audience expansion often requires changing the first playable action, not merely changing advertising.**

Transfer:
For mobile/web onboarding, offer an immediately understandable action before exposing deeper systems.

Playtest Question:
Can someone with no genre vocabulary understand the first meaningful action by watching once?

---

### 12. Control authenticity can matter more than nominal compatibility

Observation:
Modern preservation/cabinet practice shows that trackball, spinner, restricted 4-way sticks and other controls can materially change how classic games feel even when substitutes technically work.

Mechanism:
Input device geometry changes acceleration, direction constraints, precision and rhythm; a substitute can therefore alter the actual challenge.

Generalized Principle:
**Input compatibility is not equivalent to interaction equivalence.**

Transfer:
When porting to touch, measure semantic variables—turn rate, acquisition time, reversal speed, simultaneous actions—not merely whether every command has a button.

Playtest Question:
Does the new input preserve the timing and precision decisions of the original interaction model?

## Cross-source synthesis

The recurring pattern across 1970s–1990s arcade material is that the cabinet is part of the game system. Physical scale can:
- teach controls through affordance,
- expose intent to spectators,
- provide sensory state feedback,
- differentiate a venue from home play,
- change the audience that feels invited,
- create social conversation before and after a run.

The transferable lesson for current iPhone/Web projects is not to imitate arcade furniture. It is to ask which properties of the device and play context can become meaningful rules: touch geometry, orientation, haptics, short-session sharing, visible co-play and immediate onboarding.

## Promoted candidates

Strong enough for core promotion:
- physical affordance should explain a game variable
- neutral/recovery state for continuous controls
- novelty must expose repeatable skill
- input compatibility != interaction equivalence
- public-play controls should expose intent
- platform differentiation should survive graphics parity
- post-session social feedback can extend short multiplayer
- audience expansion begins at the first action

## Immediate application

### iPhone projects
- evaluate virtual controls by semantic interaction metrics, not button coverage
- make neutral state visually obvious
- avoid precision tiers based only on gesture intensity
- use haptics as state information

### Break Cars / racing
- steering feedback should expose grip/load
- gyro/virtual wheel novelty must support measurable mastery
- replay/highlight should make steering/braking intent readable

### Sky Dancer / action
- flight input should preserve reversal/acquisition timing across touch and controller
- haptic intensity should correspond to state changes, not constant spectacle

### Local / party games
- expose individual contribution inside shared goals
- create post-run social summaries beyond winner/loser
